# Craft review: “Remove Dual EC DRBG again...”

Chapter 14, openssl — Dual EC DRBG, the six-commit revert war, a suspected
backdoor told from the bodies that say *not accepted* / *now accepted*.
1,653 words of body against a 2,200–3,200 band. This is a craft review,
not a fact check. Hashes, dates, commands, and quoted output are locked.
I am not touching them.

The bones are the right bones, and they are almost in the right order. A
subject that ends in tired dots; six rows that go add / disable / disable
/ remove / revert-the-removal / remove-again; a health warning that says
*untested, probably not working*; a flag named `OPENSSL_ALLOW_DUAL_EC_DRBG`;
2,265 lines out and 2,265 lines back; eight words in a revert body; a last
object that is `make update` at 02:41. The through-line is already a
sentence the book has earned: a deletion can lose to an *acceptance*.
Three sentences in this draft are keepers for the series, and two more
will be if the certificate around them dies. I will name them.

The problem is not a third backdoor. A revert whose body names
acceptance, not a bug, is not xz and not php, and it is this chapter's
reason to exist. The problem is that the doorway issues the permission
before the six rows have been allowed to produce *again*, the hedge is
real and then immediately spent, and the narrator is sure the certificate
was the villain. 1,653 is under the floor. That is permission to sit in
the Monday the receipts contain. It is not permission to prove a lab
required Dual EC, and it is not a mandate to bronze a regeneration as a
departure.

That is the whole job.

## Does it earn its place next to xz and php?

**Yes, as archaeology. Not yet as a chapter. The new thing is a revert
that names acceptance. The draft keeps spending it as a backdoor that
compliance forced back in.**

The prompt asked this first, so I will not bury it. Chapter 04 is a
patience story: a stranger spends two and a half years becoming the
co-maintainer; the weapon is a file git was taught not to see; the tell
is one character inside a security improvement; the crime scene is the
tarball. Chapter 12 is a name story: the `Author` is a string; a revert
is a new object; the graph will not forget. A reader who has lived both
will arrive here primed to watch another backdoor. If this chapter spends
1,650 words confirming that priming — NSA, constants, Snowden, *gotcha,
they put it back* — it is xz-plus-php with a FIPS accent, and it should
not ship. If it spends them on the thing neither chapter had, it is the
reason the book gets a compliance chapter that is not a third sabotage.

That thing is already in the draft.

**The body names the obstacle.** Xz's attacker never wrote *sabotage*
into a subject; Lasse did, after. PHP's attacker wrote *typo*; Nikita
wrote *compromised git account*. Nobody in those chapters typed, as the
reason for a revert, *not accepted for 2.0.7 version of the module*, and
then, two months later, *now accepted for 2.0.8 onwards*. Those are
Henson's words. They are this chapter. They are not a lab. They are not
a checkbox. They are a maintainer telling you, in the object, that a
deletion had to be accepted, and for one version it wasn't. Protect
them. Do not spend them on a conviction the scout already failed.

**The war is in the subjects.** Add. Disable. Remove. Revert the
removal. Remove again. Six rows, one grep, one screen. Chapter 11 had a
revert that pointed at a conversation git does not contain (*as
discussed*). Chapter 12 had a revert war that was four nested *typo*s
on a Sunday. This is neither: it is one algorithm, one name, three years,
and the subjects themselves are the minutes. `git log --all --reverse
--grep='Dual EC'` is the habit. It is currently a confirmation of a
permission the doorway already announced.

**The same hand on every object.** Not Jia earning `AUTHORS`. Not a
forged Rasmus. One man adds the generator, disables it, deletes it,
puts it back, deletes it again. That is not a heist. That is a
maintainer arguing with a process in his own log. The 2017 `make update`

the cost of Dual EC, and it is not chapter 07.

What does *not* earn its place is the backdoor register, applied to a
story whose git record is an acceptance:

> As though removing a piece of code from your own project were
> something you had to get permission for. In this one case, it was —
> and the whole strange story is legible in a single screen of the
> project's own log

That sentence could open a conference talk about Dual EC. It names the
genre (permission), the verdict (it was), and the method (one screen),
before the six rows have printed. The unique archaeology is not that
OpenSSL shipped a suspected backdoor. Everyone who has heard of Dual EC
has heard that. The unique archaeology is *the deletion had to be
accepted, and he wrote that down.* That sentence is already in the
draft, in eight words, in the revert body. It is currently a caption on
a certificate.

Do not name the rhyme. Do not write "unlike xz," "unlike php," "Jia hid
it; Henson filed it." Do not write *checkbox* — that is a sneer at the

The "I might have done this" of this chapter is not the NSA, and it is
not a villainous certifier. It is Henson on 12 May 2014, putting 2,265

Sit there. Do not sit in a lab the git does not contain.

## The hedge does not hold everywhere

The prompt asked this as the critical check, so I will be exact. The git
proves *not accepted for 2.0.7* and *now accepted for 2.0.8 onwards*. It
does not prove a lab required Dual EC, that certification demanded it,
or whether the non-acceptance was substantive, procedural, or timing.
The draft knows. Here is the knowing:

> Whether the non-acceptance was substantive, procedural, or a matter
> of timing, the git does not say.

Protect that sentence. It is the honest landing of the revert. It is
also currently a throat-clearing before the chapter says what the git
does not say. Immediately after it:

> What it records, unmistakably, is the direction of authority: the
> deletion needed approval, the approval had not come, and so the code
> followed the certificate rather than the maintainer — the man
> reverting his own removal, restoring 2,265 lines he had already
> decided should not exist.

"Unmistakably." "Direction of authority." "The certificate rather than
the maintainer." That is the failed headline in softer clothes. The
commit records that the removal was not accepted for 2.0.7. It does not
record who did not accept it, or that the thing doing the not-accepting
was the certificate. *Accepted* is Henson's verb. *Certificate* is the
narrator's.

The ~ then issues the law:

> ~ A validated artifact cannot be repaired, only re-approved — which
> means, for a while, the certificate outranks the fix.

The first clause is general FIPS mechanics (a CHECK fact, see below).
The second is Dual EC causation: certificate beat fix. The git did not
say that. The hedge just said the git does not say that.

And then the rest of the chapter spends the FAIL as vocabulary. I am
flagging every sentence that overclaims the causation or implies the
maintainer was forced to keep a backdoor. These are not nits. They are

This is the FIPS explainer doing causal work. A change that *was* "not

Chooses timing among the three options the hedge left open.

"Under a compliance process" and "paperwork allowed" are the lab, the

**The fade**

The word *forced* is the overclaim in one adjective. This is the

"Overruled" is *forced* again. "In his own words" is true of *not

**At home**

The hunting tip is the right leftover, and then it invents two phrases

Starve to the phrase you actually have.

"Disagreed with" is the mind again. He may have been following a

## The three CHECK markers

**All three are genuinely external. The prose does not, except in one
grammar sneak and in the FIPS explainer's causal use, treat them as
git-proven. Keep the markers. Starve the sneak. Do not let CHECK-3 do
the work of the failed Dual EC headline.**

**CHECK 1 — Dual EC standardized in NIST SP 800-90A (2006).**

Prose: "standardized by the US government's standards body in the
mid-2000s." That is not a git claim. It does not name NIST. The subject
of the add commit names SP800-90, which is why the standard is in the
chapter at all; the year and the body are the CHECK. Correct. Keep the
marker until the fact pass.

The sneak is the sentence before the marker finishes its job:

> If someone can predict your generator's output, they can predict your
> keys, and your encryption is theatre. Dual EC was one such generator,
> standardized by the US government's standards body in the mid-2000s.

"One such generator" attaches, grammatically, to a generator whose
output can be predicted. Dual EC-as-predictable is the suspicion the
next sentences correctly mark as leaked documents and cryptographers.
Here it is a fact of grammar. Rewrite so *one such* means *a random
number generator*, or so Dual EC arrives as a generator in a standard,
not as a proven oracle. Then the suspicion. Then "You do not need to
trust any of that."

**CHECK 2 — 2013 reporting linked Dual EC to a suspected NSA backdoor;
NIST withdrew it in 2014.**

Prose: "leaked documents made public a long-standing suspicion among

later withdrew it." Then the CHECK. Then "You do not need to trust any

This is the right shape. External as news. Not git-proven. The
withdrawal is not attributed to a hash. NSA is not named in the prose.
Snowden is not named. NIST is not named. The CHECK holds the names; the
prose holds the public-record shape. That is discipline, and it is why
this is not a third sabotage chapter in the doorway. Keep it. Do not
add NSA to earn the band. Do not add Snowden. The constants and "whoever
chose them" are enough mathematics for a general reader, and an expert

Mild later leaks of CHECK-2 into git-tone:

> Fast-forward to September 2013, days after the backdoor stories broke.

Timing is git (16 September). "Days after the backdoor stories broke"

neighbor is allowed; a *because* is not. "Days after" is a neighbor.
Keep it if you will not also say he disabled it *because* of the

> in the months after the world learned what it probably was.

"Probably" is the hedge. "Learned" treats the suspicion as knowledge.

**CHECK 3 — FIPS 140-2 is the US government cryptographic-module

Prose: "The `FIPS module` is a specially packaged copy of OpenSSL's

Genuinely external. The commits keep naming *FIPS module* and *2.0.7
