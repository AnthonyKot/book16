# Giving 0 losses

In the summer of 2017, eight researchers at Google published the paper that created the
modern AI era. It described a new design for a neural network — the Transformer, the "T" in
ChatGPT — and three days after the paper went public, the code appeared on GitHub.
<!-- CHECK: "Attention Is All You Need" posted to arXiv 2017-06-12; the repo opens 2017-06-15; eight listed authors — public record. -->

The way it appeared tells you a lot. The repository's first entry is a single line — just a
title. Its second entry, filed 155 seconds later, is fifteen thousand lines of finished,
working software that already knew how to translate English into German. Not a first draft;
a delivery. The authors listed on the code: not the eight researchers, but a single entity,
*Google Inc.* Three of the paper's eight authors never appear anywhere in the repository's
history, not once. The real workshop — the months of dead ends and arguments and 2 a.m.
fixes that made the thing — lived in Google's private systems, and what the public got was
a projection on a wall: real code, true dates, and no past.

You can even catch the projector slipping. In the first week, sharp-eyed readers could find
Google's internal file paths inside the public code — the digital equivalent of a museum
exhibit still wearing its warehouse barcode. Six days in, an engineer removed them, with an
entry that says, in effect, *we've now installed a filter so the internal stuff stops
leaking out*. The filter itself stayed private. Day six is when they learned what everyone
publishing a private history learns: the projection shows the office.

## The exam

To understand the best story in this repository, you need one idea from the machine-learning
world, and it fits in a paragraph.

A language model learns by taking millions of sentences and, for each position in each
sentence, guessing the next word. Here's the catch: during training, the *whole* sentence is
sitting in memory — including the words the model is supposed to be guessing. So the code
must blindfold it: a "mask" that hides everything to the right of the position being
guessed. The mask is the exam conditions. Remove it, and the exam is open-book — the model
stops learning language and starts copying answers.

Six weeks after launch, a big routine update landed — the kind of bulk housekeeping any
maintainer waves through. Buried inside it, the masking function got a new option: the
ability to peek rightward — into the future words — for the situations where that's
legitimate. Reasonable feature. But the option was switched **on by default**, in a
function whose entire name and purpose was the blindfold. Nobody's eye snagged on it. The
next day, unrelated housekeeping moved the whole file to a new folder, so even the scene of
the change got repainted while the change was live.

Here is what that mistake looks like from the inside — and this is the part worth sitting
in. It doesn't look like a mistake. You start a training run, and the model's *loss* — the
running score of how wrong it still is — starts falling. And keeps falling. Past your last
run. Past what the paper says is possible. Toward zero, which is perfection, which no honest
model ever reaches. For an hour, maybe a day, this is the best result you have ever seen,
and everything you were ever taught says smaller loss means better model. You start drafting
the good-news message to your team.

Then a colder thought arrives, the one that separates experienced people from lucky ones:
*this is more improvement than my change can explain.*

Three days after the option landed, the person who read the score correctly was Ashish
Vaswani — the paper's first author, one of the people who put the blindfold there in the
first place. His fix removes the option entirely, and his log entry contains the sentence
this chapter is named for: the code was *"giving 0 losses, indicating that it was peeking
into the future."* A perfect score, correctly read as proof of cheating. He wrote the
diagnosis into the permanent record, where the next person staring at a miraculous
too-good curve would find it. In this kind of model, a perfect score is never mastery.
It's a leak.

## The two rulings

The repository's other great story is really two stories, decided in opposite ways, and
together they explain something true about all software.

**June 2017, week three.** One of the paper's authors, Noam Shazeer, finds a real flaw in
the code that chops text into tokens. His fix opens with a confession, right in the title:
*"This breaks existing models and vocabularies."* Anyone who had trained a model in the
library's first two weeks had to start over. Nobody hesitated. Correctness won — because
the world's total investment in this code was sixteen days old.

**December 2019.** An outside contributor reads the "positional encoding" — the formula
that tells the model where each word sits in a sentence — and notices the counting starts at
zero, and sends a one-line, mathematically sensible cleanup: count from one instead.
Nothing crashes. Every test passes. It's merged, and for nine days it sits there being,
by every visible measure, fine.

Then Google's automation rolls it back with an explanation that deserves to be famous:

> *This change will break existing checkpoints in a hard to debug, hard to notice manner.
> Existing checkpoints will still load and run, but produce entirely incorrect results.*

By late 2019, thousands of trained models existed in the world, and each had learned —
baked into millions of numerical weights — to expect the position signal exactly where the
old counting put it. Change the counting and those models don't crash. They keep running,
politely, and silently stop meaning anything. So the very project that had cheerfully broken
everyone's models at week three refused a one-line improvement at year two and a half.
Nothing about the code had changed sides. What changed was the world around it: the spec
was no longer the paper. The spec was the accumulated weight of everything everyone had
already trained. The spec had shipped.

## The empty room

After 2019 the repository winds down: from 2,228 entries in its peak year to four in its
last. The founding researchers stop, one by one. The final human act of the project's
creator, Lukasz Kaiser, was changing a single word in the description: "maintenance mode"
became "deprecated." The final entry of all, in 2023, at 3:18 in the morning on April 1st,
is an automated system bumping the copyright year — machinery stamping dates on a finished
building.

And one small thing is still in there, unchanged since a Sunday night in April 2018. Deep
in the training code sits a note Noam Shazeer wrote to himself: *"TODO(noam): ask rsepassi
for help here."* Ryan Sepassi — the engineer who pushed the very first commit — left the
project in early 2019. Shazeer left that September. The note is still at the top of the
current version, waiting. He never asked, or the answer isn't there.

If you write software, take one habit from this dig: search your project's history for the
names of its safety mechanisms — the mask, the check, the guard, whatever keeps your system
honest — because the change that loosens a guard is almost never labeled as being about the
guard. It rides inside the day's routine work, switched on by a default nobody reads. And
the next time one of your own numbers improves more than your change can explain, hold the
good-news message a little longer, and ask what your code can suddenly see.
