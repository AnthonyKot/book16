# One character

There is a piece of software you have never heard of running on almost every computer that matters.
It compresses things — squeezes files smaller — and it sits deep inside the machinery that unpacks
your operating system, your software updates, the containers that run half the internet. It is
called xz. And for essentially its entire life it was maintained by one man, working alone, for
free, in his spare time.

Keep that last part in mind, because it is not a weakness someone was foolish to allow. It is the
normal condition of an enormous amount of the software the world runs on. Somewhere behind almost
everything you use is a person doing it alone, unpaid, out of stubbornness and pride. This is a
story about what happens when someone decides to exploit exactly that.

## The two-year friendship

In early 2022 a new contributor showed up and started helping. Not with anything flashy — with
exactly the boring, careful work an overloaded solo maintainer prays for. Small defensive fixes.
Test cleanups. Tidying. The kind of contributions that say *I am reliable and I am not going
anywhere.*

The maintainer, worn down by years of carrying the thing alone, was grateful. Over months the
newcomer — using the name Jia Tan — sent more and better work, and the maintainer did the natural
thing: he wrote the new name into the file that says who the project belongs to. A co-maintainer.
By the next year, Jia was doing more of the work than the founder was.

Nobody in that room was watching a robbery. They were watching a burnout end. And that is the whole
point of the two and a half years that followed — not the code, the *trust*. What was being built,
patiently, was not a program. It was permission. The eventual sabotage could never have been slipped
in by a stranger; it had to come from the co-maintainer. And the only way to become the
co-maintainer was to be genuinely, verifiably helpful for a very long time.

## The weapon that isn't there

Now the strange part. When the attack was finally discovered, people rushed to the project's history
to look at the malicious code — and it wasn't there. You cannot find it. It was never filed into the
ledger at all.

Here is the trick, and it is worth slowing down for, because it is the cleverest thing in the story.

Software is written and edited in one place — the repository, the ledger of every change, which
everyone can inspect. But that is not the thing people actually download and install. What they
install is a *package*, built from the repository and bundled up for shipping. Most of the time the
two are the same. The attack lived precisely in the gap between them: the malicious code was slipped
into the shipped package, but never into the repository everyone was watching.

And there is one tiny, damning fingerprint of that plan left behind. Nine days before the poisoned
version shipped, Jia added a single line to a housekeeping file — the list of files git should
*ignore* and never mention. The line named the very file the weapon would travel in.

Think about what that does. It tells the tool everyone trusts to show them what changed: *if this
particular file ever appears, say nothing.* The maintainer could regenerate his project, the
poisoned file could sit right there on disk, and the status would come back clean. The one line was
so innocent-looking — just tidying an ignore-list — that after the whole thing blew up, the founder
himself, not yet understanding, copied it forward twice because in isolation it looked exactly like
good hygiene.

If you take one habit from this chapter: *what you ship is not the same thing as what you wrote down.*
The gap between them is where this lived. Compare the two, and look hard at anything in the shipped
version that isn't in the ledger.

## The period

Then there is the character the chapter is named for.

In early 2024 Jia added what looked like a safety improvement — a check to see whether the program
could take advantage of a protective feature in the operating system, a kind of cage that limits the
damage a compromised program can do. Adding a cage is exactly what a conscientious maintainer does.
The commit was titled like ordinary, responsible work.

To run the check, the code has to compile a tiny test program. And sitting in the middle of that
test program, on a line by itself, was a single period. A dot. Where no dot belongs.

That dot is a syntax error. It means the little test program cannot compile. Which means the check
always fails. Which means the operating system concludes the protective cage "isn't available." Which
means the program quietly runs without its cage — wide open — while every log says everything is
fine. The same commit made the same change in a second place, the ordinary way, *without* the dot.
Only the one that mattered was broken.

~ Someone sat in front of that file and added one character.

It stayed there, patiently switching off the safety, until long after the attack was public. When
the founder finally fixed it, the entire fix was that one character, deleted — and for the first
time the record said out loud what had happened. The word he used was *sabotaged*. A single dot,
removed. The sabotage and the cure were the very same character, pointing opposite directions.

## Stopped by half a second

What that dot was protecting is why this is not a footnote. The hidden code, once it reached the
machines it was aiming for, would have quietly rewired the part of the system that guards the front
door of a server — the gatekeeper that decides who is allowed in. A compromised gatekeeper is not a
leak of some data. It is a master key to a huge share of the internet's servers, delivered straight
through the update channel those servers trust the most. The poisoned versions were already sitting
in the test branches of the major systems, days away from going out to everyone.

It was caught by an accident. An engineer at another company noticed that logging into his own
machines was taking about half a second longer than it should. That was all — half a second of
unexplained slowness. He was annoyed enough to go looking for why, followed the delay down into the
compression library, and came out the other side holding the backdoor. He made it public in late
March 2024.

The distance between a historical curiosity and the worst supply-chain attack ever shipped was one
tired, careful person being irritated by half a second.

## What to read twice

The hardest lesson of xz is that nothing was asleep. The code was reviewed. The maintainer was
diligent. The contributor was, by every visible sign, excellent — for years — because being
excellent for years *was* the attack. You could not have caught this by scanning for sloppiness. One
at a time, the malicious changes were indistinguishable from good ones, and the two that mattered
most were a tidy little ignore-list edit and a stray dot.

But two habits would have narrowed it, and they cost nothing. First: what you ship is not what you
wrote down — compare them, and stare at anything extra in the shipped copy. Second: read the
*negative* side of every improvement. A change that adds a safety cage is also a change that can
quietly disable one, and the difference can be a single character that no search for suspicious words
will ever find, because the description says the exact opposite of what the change does. Blame here
is not about who to punish. It is about which line to read twice.
